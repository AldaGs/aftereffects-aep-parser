# Why Veracruz Lets Its Coffee Go Sour: Script and Storyboard

**Status:** draft v2 (revised against `notes/fact_check.md`, `notes/visual_direction.md` and `notes/story_edit.md`). The working title in the series plan was "Washed, Natural, Honey". See the review decisions in the build notes.
**Format:** 4:09 (615 words of VO ≈ 3:58 at 155 wpm, plus about 11 s of holds), 1920×1080, 30 fps, AE 26.5.
**The question:** Why does Veracruz's coffee law mention letting the beans ferment?
**The answer:** Fermenting does very little to the bean itself. Under the pulp, each seed is coated in mucilage, a slime that water won't rinse off. In the tank, microbes eat its sugars and the water sours, and as it sours the slime comes loose so it can be washed away. The seed is then dried from about half water to 10–12 %.
**The surprise:** Nothing in the tank digests the slime. A French–Mexican team working on Coatepec coffee found the pectin barely touched after 20 hours, and the tank's main bacterium made no enzyme that cuts pectin. The slime's release tracked the souring. Lands in scene 6 (2:13), just past the middle.
**Mexico's part:** The experiment that overturned the "microbes digest the pectin" story was run on coffee from Coatepec, Veracruz (1998 harvest), the same region whose designation of origin writes the fermenting step into law. The cost lands in Mexico too: the runoff, *aguas mieles*, sours rivers, and the machines that save the water skip the very fermentation the law credits.
**Reused from earlier episodes:** the red cherry and its cross-section (ep. 1, S1), with the inner layers (mucilage, parchment, silverskin) added once in `coffee_common.py`. Also reused: the Mexico map with state outlines (ep. 1, S7), the document recreation, the gauge/needle dial and the Coatepec dot (ep. 3, S1/S7).
**New colours:** ferment magenta `#B4457F` (microbes and the acid they make), from the locked season palette. Water is water cyan `#4FB3C8` (season palette). Mucilage is a white fill with an ink outline. Honey grades use dried-mucilage tones from the seed/pulp object colours, never arabica red.

## Logline

Veracruz's designation of origin credits a farm habit, fermenting freshly pulped coffee, for the acidity in the cup. A cherry, cut open layer by layer, shows the problem every method has to solve: a slime that won't rinse off. The episode puts the methods on the one scale they share, how much fruit stays on while the seed dries, and then follows a washed seed through the pulper and into the tank, where yeasts and bacteria multiply and the water sours until the slime lets go. Past the middle, a French–Mexican experiment on Coatepec coffee shows the old explanation was wrong: nothing in the tank eats the pectin. The seed dries. Mexico pays for washing in water, and its water-saving machines skip the step its law celebrates. The close goes back to the law, read correctly.

## Script and storyboard

Timecodes are for the master comp.

### 1. The law (0:00–0:23, `S1_Declaration`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** White frame. A flat recreation of the Diario Oficial page types on: DECLARATORIA DE PROTECCIÓN DE LA DENOMINACIÓN DE ORIGEN "CAFÉ VERACRUZ". Credit lower-left: `DIARIO OFICIAL DE LA FEDERACIÓN, 15 NOV 2000`.
- **B:** The camera pushes in to one clause. Two highlighter swipes draw in turn: first on *altura* (altitude), then on *fermentar el café recién despulpado*. A small leader line beside the second: = FERMENTING FRESHLY PULPED COFFEE. The rest of the page clears. The second swipe's end cap shrinks into a dot and turns red. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "In 2000, Mexico gave Veracruz coffee a protected designation of origin, the way France protects Champagne. The declaration credits the coffee's acidity to two things. One is altitude, which is a story of its own. The other is a farm habit: letting the coffee ferment right after it's pulped. What is fermenting doing to a coffee bean?"

### 2. Inside the cherry (0:23–0:52, `S2_Layers`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The red dot grows into the ep. 1 cherry, which splits into a cross-section. Leader lines with dots draw out to each layer, outside in, one at a time: SKIN, PULP, MUCILAGE, PARCHMENT, SILVERSKIN, SEED.
- **B:** The mucilage ring (white fill, ink outline) gets a highlighter swipe. A small composition bar draws beside it: WATER ≈ 85–91 %, with the thin remainder labelled SUGARS ≈ 6–7 % and PECTIN AND OTHER†. Source line: `SOURCE: CENICAFÉ`.
- **C:** The skin and pulp slide away, leaving the seed in its mucilage ring at centre. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "Start with the cherry. Under the red skin is a sweet pulp. Under that is a layer of slime called mucilage, clinging to a papery shell, the parchment. Inside the parchment, wrapped in a thin silver skin, is the seed. Everything outside the seed has to go, and the mucilage is the hard part. It's about nine parts water. Most of the rest is sugar and pectin, the stuff that sets jam."

### 3. How much fruit stays on (0:52–1:26, `S3_FruitAxis`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The seed in its mucilage ring sits at the centre of a horizontal axis that draws outwards in both directions: FRUIT LEFT ON THE SEED WHILE IT DRIES, from NONE to ALL.
- **B:** Seeds drop onto the axis at their positions, with no separate rows or chips. A whole cherry at ALL darkens to a dried husk beside a small calendar running ≈ 3–4 WEEKS†: NATURAL, THE OLDEST METHOD†. Then four honey seeds, each placed along the axis by how much mucilage it keeps, from a thin pale coat near NONE to a thick dark one near ALL, in dried-mucilage tones: WHITE, YELLOW, RED and BLACK HONEY†. At NONE, a bare parchment seed: WASHED. Source line: `SOURCE: SCA; ICAFE`.
- **C:** In the same precomp, a short second axis grows straight down from the middle of the first: OXYGEN, from OPEN at the top to SEALED at the bottom. One seed slides down it into a small sealed-tank outline: ANAEROBIC. The fruit axis doesn't move.
- **D:** The camera slides to the WASHED seed at the NONE end. *(Match cut out on the seed.)*

**VO:** "There's more than one way to deal with the slime. Picture a scale: how much of the fruit stays on the seed while it dries. Natural coffee, the oldest method, dries inside the whole cherry. Honey coffee keeps some of the mucilage, from a thin coat to nearly all of it. Newer methods, like fermenting in sealed tanks, change the air instead and need a scale of their own. Washed coffee keeps none of the fruit, and that's the end Veracruz's law has in mind."

### 4. The pulper (1:26–1:42, `S4_Pulper`, mechanism step 1)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The washed seed regrows its white mucilage ring and drops out of the chute of a drum pulper, which draws on around it in flat side view. Cherries roll in at the top. Skins and pulp drop out one side, and coated seeds drop out the chute below.
- **B:** A water-cyan stream pours over a seed and the mucilage ring stays on. Label: WATER ALONE WON'T SHIFT IT. The seed falls towards a tank's water line. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "On a washed farm, a pulping machine squeezes the seeds out of their skins on the day they're picked. The mucilage comes through with them, and water alone won't rinse it off. So the seeds go into a tank."

### 5. The tank (1:42–2:13, `S5_Tank`, mechanism step 2)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* A tank in cross-section, drawn as an ink outline about 800 × 420 px on white, with one water-cyan water line near its top and a pile of cream seed shapes below it (no water fill, no concrete texture). A clock in the corner runs from 0 to 20 HOURS.
- **B:** Magenta microbes appear on the seeds themselves, one group at a time, each with its label, as pre-placed dot groups with staggered opacity. Small ink-outlined dots are OTHER BACTERIA, the first and most numerous. Magenta circles are YEASTS and magenta rods are LACTIC ACID BACTERIA, the two groups that multiply fastest, with a tiny yoghurt-pot icon beside the rods. A sugar meter on the tank wall drains from 100 to about 40: ≈ 60 % OF THE SIMPLE SUGARS EATEN, ALL MICROBES TOGETHER. Source line: `SOURCE: AVALLONE ET AL. 2001`.
- **C:** The sugar meter goes guide grey. A pH gauge beside the tank swings from about 5 towards 4. Two reference marks sit on its scale: MILK near 6.7 and TOMATO JUICE near 4.2†. The needle settles by the tomato. Source line: `SOURCE: AVALLONE ET AL. 2002; FDA pH TABLE`.
- **D:** The camera pushes past the gauge into the tank, to a fistful of seeds being lifted out. It is two static hand silhouettes swapped on a cut: open, with the seeds sliding out (SLICK, 6 HOURS), then closed, holding their shape (GRITTY, 20 HOURS†). One magenta dot is left on a seed in the closed hand. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "For about a day, the tank fills with life. Yeasts and lactic acid bacteria, relatives of the ones that sour yoghurt, multiply fastest. Together, the microbes eat about sixty percent of the simple sugars, and as they eat they make acid. The water goes from mildly sour to about as sour as tomato juice. Farmers check by hand. Early on, the seeds slip through their fingers. When they feel gritty, like wet gravel, the slime has let go."

### 6. What the tank really does (2:13–2:54, `S6_Acid`, the turn)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The magenta dot grows into a document card: *Avallone, Guiraud, Guyot, Olguín & Brillouet, Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, 2001*. A small inset map tag drops next to it: COATEPEC, VERACRUZ, 1998 HARVEST, with the ep. 3 Coatepec dot in Mexico green.
- **B:** A two-bar chart builds: PECTIN IN THE MUCILAGE, BEFORE and AFTER 20 h. The two bars are nearly the same height. Label: BARELY TOUCHED. The bacterium icon, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, gets a crossed-out pair of scissors: MADE NO PECTIN-CUTTING ENZYME. Source line: `SOURCE: AVALLONE ET AL. 2001`.
- **C:** Back in the tank, the pH needle and the mucilage ring sit on one shared clock. As the needle falls, the ring loosens and lifts off the parchment. Two small leader lines from the ring name the authors' candidate causes: ACID and WATER DRAWN OUT AS SUGAR IS EATEN. A third, smaller label: EXACT MECHANISM: UNKNOWN. Source line: `SOURCE: AVALLONE ET AL. 2002`.
- **D:** The bacterium shrinks back to a dot and drops into a channel of running water. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "For decades, the explanation was that the microbes digest the pectin, the way fruit rots. Around 2000, a French and Mexican team tested that on coffee from Coatepec. After twenty hours in the tank, the pectin was mostly still there. The bacterium that took over their tank, Leuconostoc, made no enzyme that cuts pectin. What tracked the slime coming loose was the acid. The team had two guesses why, the acid itself or water drawn out of the slime as its sugar disappears, and they called the exact cause unknown. The tank doesn't eat the mucilage. It sours, and the slime lets go."

### 7. Washing and drying (2:54–3:16, `S7_Dry`, mechanism step 3)

### 8. The price in water (3:16–3:51, `S8_Water`)
**Storyboard**

**VO:** "Mexico's export coffee is mostly washed, and washing drinks water. A traditional mill can use up to about twenty litres for every kilo of cherry. The runoff, which Mexicans call aguas mieles, honey water, is sour and full of sugar, and as it rots in a stream it uses up the oxygen fish need. Some Mexican mills now scrub the mucilage off by machine, with about three and a half litres. But a machine skips the tank, and the tank is the step the law is proud of."

**VO:** "Fermenting does very little to the bean itself. It sours the water around it, and the slime comes loose. The law gives the tank credit for the acidity in your cup. The science can only vouch for the slime."

## Notes for the build

- **One cherry cross-section** (the shared asset) with the inner layers as concentric shape groups. Group order, top first: seed, silverskin, parchment, mucilage, pulp, skin.
- **The two axes (S3)** live in one precomp. The fruit axis stays horizontal along the top. The oxygen axis grows straight down from its middle (a trim-path draw), so nothing rotates and the dots on the fruit axis stay put. This replaces v1's "tilt 90°", which would have rotated the dots.
- **Honey seeds (S3)** sit along the fruit axis at their mucilage amount. Their fills step through the object colours (`#F1E1BE` → `#C9A56E` → `#5B4A2E` range), never arabica red.
- **Microbes (S5)** are pre-placed dot groups on the seeds, generated in Python, with staggered opacity keyed to the clock. A count-driven repeater would line them up in rows off the seeds.
- **The pH gauge (S5, S6)** is one needle rotation driven by a slider. S6 reuses the same slider to drive the mucilage ring's offset, so the two move together on one clock.
- **The hand (S5 D)** is two static silhouettes swapped on a cut, not an articulated hand.
- **The scale (S7)** display is a source-text expression on the same slider as the patio's day counter: `100 - 44 * t`.
- **The declaration (S1, S9)** is a flat typographic recreation, credited to the DOF, not a scan.
- **Colours:** ferment magenta only for microbes, acid and the *aguas mieles* runoff. Water cyan for clean water. Arabica red only on cherries. Mexico green on Mexican tags and the Coatepec dot.
- **White-frame pass:** the S5 tank is an ink outline with a water line, not a filled tank (cut 4→5 wording updated); microbe groups arrive one at a time and the sugar meter greys before the pH gauge; the S7 channel and patio are lines; the S8 map greys while the bottles build.
- **Fonts:** Chonburi-Regular, Georgia-Italic, ArialMT and Arial-BoldMT.

## Facts to check (†)

- **Café Veracruz designation of origin. It credits altitude and "la arraigada tradición de fermentar el café recién despulpado" for its acidity. Signed 6 Nov 2000, published in the DOF on 15 Nov 2000.** Confirmed (WIPO Lex). The screen shows the DOF date.
- **Mucilage ≈ 85–91 % water and ≈ 6.2–7.4 % sugars.** Cenicafé. Plausible per the fact check, not re-checked.
- **Layer order: skin, pulp, mucilage, parchment, silverskin, seed.** Standard anatomy.
- **Natural ≈ 3–4 weeks drying; natural is the oldest method.** Widely repeated. Confirm with a processing reference.
- **Honey grades white, yellow, red and black, by mucilage amount and the colour it dries to.** Costa Rican mill practice, and not standardised. Confirm with ICAFE or a named mill.
- **Anaerobic fermentation in sealed tanks.** A current specialty practice. No date or figure is shown.
- **≈ 20 h fermentation; ≈ 60 % of simple sugars degraded by the total microflora (mostly Gram-negative bacilli such as *Erwinia* and *Klebsiella*); yeasts and lactic acid bacteria showed the largest growth.** Avallone et al. 2001, *Current Microbiology* 42: 252 (fact check).
- **pH from about 5 to about 4; tomato juice ≈ 4.2, milk ≈ 6.7.** Avallone et al. 2002, *Int. J. Food Sci. Tech.*: fermentation conditions at pH 5.3–3.5. Reference values from the FDA food pH table.
- **"Gritty, like wet gravel" as the hand test.** Common in washed-coffee guides. Confirm with a Mexican *beneficio* manager or an AMECAFÉ manual.
- **Coffee from the Coatepec area (Xalapa, Veracruz), *C. arabica* var. Typica, 1998 harvest, 20 h tank, Penagos pulper; Olguín at INECOL, Xalapa.** Confirmed (Avallone et al., histology paper, Methods).
- **Pectin "no apparent degradation" after 20 h ("restricted pectolysis").** Confirmed (PubMed 11714359).
- ***Leuconostoc mesenteroides* dominant, no pectolytic enzymes.** Confirmed for this study's strains. Other studies report pectinolytic strains, hence "their tank" and "made no enzyme".
- **Two candidate mechanisms (acid-driven cell-wall changes; osmotic pressure as microbes eat the sugars); mechanism "unknown"; removal tracks acidification.** Avallone group (fact check, 2002 abstract).
- **Fresh washed parchment ≈ 50 % water; 10–12 % target; 100 g → ≈ 56 g.** SCA target. The starting figure is secondary (45–55 %). 56 g is arithmetic (50 g ÷ 0.89).
- **Most Mexican export grades are washed.** UV-IIESCA 2004 (the predominant export grades are all *lavado*). Confirm a current share with AMECAFÉ/SIAP. For volumes, use USDA FAS *Mexico: Coffee Annual*, May 2026 (MX2026-0026), not the 2024 edition.
- **Traditional washed mill up to ≈ 20 L/kg cherry; Mexican semi-washed with mechanical demucilaging 3.4 L/kg.** Wikipedia "Coffee wastewater" table (Von Enden & Calvert 2002, Vietnam; Bello-Mendoza & Castillo-Rivera 1998, Mexico). Secondary. Find a Mexican traditional-mill figure.
- **Runoff pH ≈ 4; organic load uses up oxygen in streams.** Wikipedia "Coffee wastewater" (Bello-Mendoza et al. 1995, Soconusco mills). Confirm.
- **"Some Mexican mills now scrub the mucilage off by machine."** Supported only by the 1998 study. Confirm how common demucilagers are in Veracruz today.
