# Who Gets Paid for Your Coffee: Script and Storyboard

**Status:** draft v2 (revised against `notes/fact_check.md`, `notes/visual_direction.md` and `notes/story_edit.md`).
**Format:** 4:42 (723 words of VO), 1920×1080, 30 fps, AE 26.5.
**The question:** Of the five dollars you pay for a latte, how much reaches the person who grew the coffee, and who decides?
**The answer:** About fifteen cents is the coffee, priced per pound by futures traders in New York (the "C price"), and the farmer gets roughly eight to ten of those cents after the exporter, the local buyer and the exchange rate. From 1963 to 1989 a treaty's quotas held that price up, on and off; since 1989, in Mexico, nothing does except a floor that a few cooperatives built.
**The surprise:** In the turn (scenes 6–7): the treaty's quotas had been suspended twice before, from 1972 to 1980 and from 1986 to 1987, while coffee was dear, and both times they came back. In July 1989 they went and never returned, and the average price over the next five years was little more than half the average of the five before. Then (scene 9): the first fair-trade label, the floor that took the treaty's place for some farmers, started with a Mexican cooperative months before the treaty broke.
**Mexico's part:** Mexico lost two floors in the same year: the treaty and INMECAFE, the state buyer. And Mexico's farmers wrote the replacement. Without Mexico, the episode ends on a market; with it, it ends on who built a floor under that market.
**Reused from earlier episodes:** the ep. 1 cherry (close), the Mexico map with state outlines, the world map, the ep. 2 farm plot with football pitches and its Chiapas dot. The coyote truck (S4) is new here and goes into the common module for ep. 10.
**New colours:** none. Prices are drawn in ink (season palette). Mexico green marks what Mexican farmers receive and the UCIRI cooperative. The ICA quota corridor is guide grey.

## Logline

A number ticking on a screen in New York sets the price of the coffee in a five-dollar latte, which comes to about fifteen cents. We follow those fifteen cents from the exchange back up the hill to a two-hectare farm in Chiapas, losing a slice at every stop, and find the farmer's share. Then we learn that a treaty used to hold the price up, that it stepped aside twice when coffee was dear and came back, and that when it broke on 4 July 1989 it never returned. Mexico lost its state coffee buyer the same year. The floor that replaced them started in a cooperative in Oaxaca.

## Script and storyboard

Timecodes are for the master comp.

### 1. Cold open: a number in New York (0:00–0:20, `S1_Ticker`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** White frame. A single ticker line in ink, generic board style, scrolls left to right: **COFFEE "C" ≈ 295 ¢/LB†**. The digits tick every second. Under it, a one-pound bag outline gives the unit. `SOURCE: ICE FUTURES U.S., 7 OCT 2026`.
- **B:** A thin leader line drops from the ticker across a small Mexico silhouette to a Chiapas dot in Mexico green.
- **C:** A flat latte cup on a saucer draws on beside it, with a receipt: **LATTE ≈ $5.00†** (illustrative US café price). `SOURCE: SQUARE CAFÉ PRICE DATA, 2026`.
- **D:** The cup turns into a tall stacked bar of the same height, all light grey, labelled $5.00. A thin ink sliver grows in at the bottom: **≈ 15¢: THE COFFEE**. The camera pushes in on the sliver. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "This number changes every few seconds on a screen in New York. It's the price of a pound of coffee, in cents, and it sets what a family in Chiapas is paid for its harvest. A latte costs about five dollars.† How much of that reaches the farmer, and who decides?"

### 2. Twenty-two grams (0:20–0:44, `S2_Grams`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The sliver becomes the rim of a scale pan. Roasted beans pour onto it and the readout stops at **18 g**. Beside it, a second pan of green coffee in ep. 1's cream seed colour settles at **22 g**. A label between them: THE ROAST TAKES ABOUT A SIXTH OF THE WEIGHT†. `SOURCE: ILLY & VIANI, *ESPRESSO COFFEE* (2005)`.
- **B:** A tag on the green pan: **≈ $3.00 / LB, OCT 2026†** resolves to **≈ 15¢**. The $5.00 bar returns as a strip of 100 small squares; 3 of them fill ink: **3 %**. `SOURCE: ICE FUTURES U.S.`. *(Match cut out on the three squares.)*

**VO:** "The coffee in a latte is about eighteen grams of roasted beans, which were about twenty-two grams of green coffee before the roast burned off about a sixth of their weight.† In October 2026, a pound of green arabica traded for about three dollars in New York.† Twenty-two grams of it comes to about fifteen cents, three percent of your five dollars."

### 3. The C price (0:44–1:07, `S3_Exchange`, chain stop 1)
**Storyboard.** The mechanism runs through scenes 3 and 4 on one numbered rail along the bottom of the frame. Its stops are the places the price passes through, four of them: **1 EXCHANGE → 2 EXPORTER → 3 COYOTE → 4 FARM**. The differential and the exchange rate are not stops; they ride on the bar as a tag and a dial. Stops are not spoken as numbers.
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The squares merge into a dot that draws a jagged ink line across a flat trading board. Label: COFFEE "C" FUTURES · ICE · NEW YORK. Rail stop 1 fills.
- **B:** A trader icon passes a contract slip to another; neither slip nor trader ever touches a sack (a sack sits greyed out of reach).
- **C:** A small Brazil inset. A frost icon appears and the line jumps; a full-harvest icon appears and the line falls. A leader line runs from the falling line to a Chiapas dot, which dips with it.
- **D:** The line flattens into a horizontal bar at the top of the frame. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "That price is called the C price. It's set on the ICE futures exchange in New York, where traders buy and sell contracts for coffee to be delivered months from now. Most of them never touch a bean. When Brazil has a frost, the price jumps. When Brazil has a big harvest, it falls, and it falls in Chiapas too."

### 4. The trip back up the hill (1:07–1:51, `S4_Chain`, chain stops 2–4)
**Storyboard.** A horizontal route axis across the frame: NEW YORK on the left, then VERACRUZ PORT and a DRY MILL (the exporter), a ROADHEAD (the coyote), and a HILLSIDE in Chiapas on the right. The ink price bar runs above it at full width. At each stop a piece drops off the bar's right end.
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* Rail stop 2. A ± tag on the bar: C ± DIFFERENTIAL, with a chip: **MEXICO: AT PAR ON THE EXCHANGE†**. `SOURCE: ICE COFFEE "C" RULES`. A second chip under it: A REAL SHIPMENT: ABOVE OR BELOW, BY QUALITY.
- **B:** At the port and the mill, the exporter's piece drops off, split into four hatched segments of **equal width**, each marked **?**: MILLING · SHIPPING · FINANCE · MARGIN. The hatching says the sizes are unknown.
- **C:** Rail stop 3. At the roadhead, a pickup-truck icon labelled COYOTE takes one more hatched **?** segment. A dashed path continues to the hillside where the road ends.
- **D:** Rail stop 4. A $ → MXN dial sits at the hillside. It turns towards a stronger peso and the remaining bar shrinks while nothing upstream moves.
- **E:** The route axis, its stop labels, the tags and the dial go guide grey. The remainder turns Mexico green, unhatched. Six sacks of cherries (ep. 1 cherry as the icon) pour into one sack of green coffee: **≈ 6 KG CHERRY → 1 KG GREEN†**; the sacks slide off before the next beat. The green remainder sits against a ghost of the full bar: **≈ ½ TO ⅔ OF THE NEW YORK PRICE (2020)†**. `SOURCE: SIAP PRECIO MEDIO RURAL, CAFÉ CEREZA 2020; ICE; BANXICO`. The 15¢ sliver appears below at the same scale with **≈ 8–10¢** filled green inside it. The green remainder shrinks to a dot on Chiapas. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "Then the price travels back up the hill, and something comes off at every stop. An exporter buys each shipment at the C price, plus or minus a differential. On the exchange, Mexican coffee counts at par; a real shipment sells above or below that, depending on quality.† The exporter takes out milling, shipping, financing and a margin. A local buyer, the coyote, collects from farms the trucks can't reach. And the farmer is paid in pesos, per kilo of cherries, about six kilos for each kilo of green coffee.† Official farm prices for 2020 came to roughly half to two-thirds of the New York price.† Of your fifteen cents, that's about eight to ten."

### 5. One farm, two prices (1:51–2:16, `S5_Swing`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The Chiapas dot grows into the ep. 2 farm plot: **2 HA**, with the three football pitches beside it. Twelve sacks stack next to the plot: **≈ 700 KG GREEN A YEAR†**. `SOURCE: USDA FAS MX2026-0026 (≈ 0.36 T/HA)`.
- **B:** A C-price chart, 1990–2026, draws on to the right in ink. At the 2001 low, a marker: **< 50¢ / LB†**. The twelve sacks turn into a short green bar: **≈ $770**. `SOURCE: ICE; ICO`. The line climbs to February 2025, marker **≈ $4 / LB (MONTHLY AVERAGE)†**, and the green bar grows on the same axis to **≈ $6,100**, about eight times as tall. A chip under both bars: AT THE NEW YORK PRICE, BEFORE ANY DEDUCTIONS. The plot stays still while only the line moves. *(Match cut out on the chart.)*

**VO:** "A two-hectare farm in Chiapas grows about seven hundred kilos of green coffee a year,† roughly twelve sacks. At the 2001 low, under fifty cents a pound, that crop was worth about seven hundred and seventy dollars at the New York price.† In February 2025, at about four dollars, it was worth more than six thousand.† The trees didn't change. New York did."

### 6. The turn: a treaty that came and went (2:16–2:48, `S6_Treaty`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The chart's axis slides left to start at 1962. **The document on screen:** a flat recreation of the 1962 agreement's title page, lower-left, with a highlighter swipe on "export quotas". Credit: `INTERNATIONAL COFFEE AGREEMENT, 1962, RECREATED`.
- **B:** The document slides off. A guide-grey corridor draws along the ink line from 1963, labelled QUOTAS IN FORCE. On the right edge, a vertical stacked bar shows world exports divided into producer quotas: Brazil's slice labelled as the largest, Mexico's in green, the rest grey. When the line dips towards the corridor's floor, the bar shrinks. The VO's Cold War line plays over this bar; there is no separate inset.
- **C:** The quota bar slides off the right edge. The corridor breaks open in two places. Each gap is labelled with its dates on the time axis: **DEC 1972 – OCT 1980†** and **FEB 1986 – OCT 1987†**. In both gaps the ink line runs above where the corridor's ceiling would be. A frost icon sits in the first gap (1975) and a drought icon in the second (1985–86)†. `SOURCE: ICO; BATES, *OPEN-ECONOMY POLITICS* (1997)`.
- **D:** After each gap the corridor redraws. A highlighter swipe lands on the second redraw: CAME BACK.

**VO:** "Governments once tried to take that swing away. From 1963, a treaty, the International Coffee Agreement, gave each exporting country a quota. When the price fell, quotas tightened and less coffee reached the market. The United States signed partly to keep Latin America's coffee economies steady during the Cold War.† But the quotas ran on and off. They were suspended from 1972 to 1980, and again from 1986 to 1987, both times while frost or drought kept coffee dear.† Both times, they came back."

### 7. 4 July 1989 (2:48–3:11, `S7_Crash`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* A date chip types on at 1989: **4 JULY 1989†**. The corridor cracks at that point and its pieces drop off the bottom of the frame. The quota bar slides back in on the right, splits and its pieces spill, then it leaves.
- **B:** The ink line falls. Two flat average bars sit on the same price axis, one over the five years before and one over the five years after: **≈ $1.34** and **≈ 77¢†**, the second a little over half the first. `SOURCE: ICO INDICATOR PRICE, FIVE-YEAR AVERAGES`. No corridor redraws. The camera pushes in on the 1989 point, now marked by an ink ring. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "In 1989 the members couldn't agree on new quotas, and on the fourth of July they suspended them again.† Coffee that had been held back poured onto the market. Over the next five years the price averaged about seventy-seven cents a pound, against a dollar thirty-four in the five years before.† This time, the quotas never came back."

### 8. Mexico loses its buyer (3:11–3:39, `S8_Inmecafe`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The ring is the 1989 mark on a short timeline, 1958–1993, labelled INMECAFE (Instituto Mexicano del Café). A chip at 1958: FOUNDED†.
- **B:** Above the timeline, the Mexico map with state outlines. Ink dots for INMECAFE buying centres pop on across Chiapas, Veracruz, Oaxaca and Puebla. A bar beside the map fills to **≈ 44 % OF THE CROP BOUGHT, 1982–83†** against an outline at 100 %. `SOURCE: UAM, *SOCIOLÓGICA*`.
- **C:** At 1989 the dots start to vanish, a few at a time; at 1993 the last goes: **LIQUIDATED 1993†**. `SOURCE: *LIMINAR* (CESMECA)`.
- **D:** Only the coyote truck icons and an ink C-price line along the top are left on the map. The camera finds one green dot on the Isthmus of Tehuantepec. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "Mexico lost a second floor the same year. Since 1958, a state agency, INMECAFE, had advised growers, and by the early 1980s it bought more than forty percent of the crop.† In 1989 the government began pulling it out of the market, and by 1993 it was gone.† A family on two hectares now had no treaty above it and no state buyer beside it. It had the coyote and the C price."

### 9. A floor from Oaxaca (3:39–4:20, `S9_Floor`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* Push in to the green dot on the Isthmus: **UCIRI**, a cooperative of indigenous growers. A dashed line arcs from it across the Atlantic to the Netherlands.
- **B:** **The document on screen.** A flat recreation of a 1988 Dutch coffee pack (generic layout, no real logo) with a date chip: **15 NOV 1988: FIRST FAIR-TRADE LABEL†**. `SOURCE: STICHTING MAX HAVELAAR; SOLIDARIDAD`.
- **C:** Cut to the C-price chart. An ink-dashed line grows out of the green UCIRI dot and runs flat across the chart at **$1.80 / LB†**, labelled FAIRTRADE MINIMUM, WASHED ARABICA (SINCE 2023). Where the ink price line dips below it (around 2001, around 2019), the gap between them fills with light hatching. `SOURCE: FAIRTRADE INTERNATIONAL`.
- **D:** Under the chart, a strip of 100 small sacks for the world's coffee in 2023; 2 of them are outlined in ink-dash: **≈ 2 IN 100 SOLD ON FAIRTRADE TERMS†**. `SOURCE: FAIRTRADE COFFEE IMPACT 2025 (202,979 T); FAO (≈ 11 M T)`. The camera returns to the green dot at the dashed line's start. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "Some of those families had already built something. In the Isthmus of Oaxaca, a cooperative called UCIRI asked a Dutch charity for a fair price instead of aid. In November 1988 the result went on sale in the Netherlands: Max Havelaar, the first fair-trade label, with UCIRI's coffee in the pack.† It guarantees a minimum, today a dollar eighty a pound for washed arabica, however low New York goes.† In 2023, about two kilos in every hundred of the world's coffee sold that way.† It's a floor where the treaty's used to be, for the farmers who can sell on it, and Mexican farmers drew it."

### 10. Close (4:20–4:42, `S10_Close`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The green dot stretches sideways into the lower part of the 15¢ ink sliver at the bottom of the latte's $5.00 bar, at the same size as scene 1. The farmer's share is drawn **inside** the coffee's sliver: about three-fifths of it green, the rest ink. Labels: **≈ 15¢ THE COFFEE**, **≈ 8–10¢ THE FARMER†**.
- **B:** The bar turns back into the latte cup on its saucer. The sliver lifts out of it and closes into a red circle: the coffee cherry from episode 1, centred, with its stem and leaf. The cherry opens on its two seeds. The end card types on: **Who Gets Paid for Your Coffee**, with a highlighter swipe on "Paid". It holds for 2 s.

**VO:** "Most of your five dollars never leaves the café. About fifteen cents is the coffee, priced by traders in New York, and the farmer at the top of the hill gets around eight to ten of them. Since 1989, nothing holds that price up except a floor that started in Oaxaca."

## Notes for the build

- **Word count:** 723 words of VO at 155 wpm ≈ 4:40. Timecodes include the 2 s end-card hold in S10.
- **Generator:** `schema/examples/who_gets_paid.py`, on `schema/examples/coffee_common.py` (palette, maps, chart rig, step rail, 100-cell strip, cherry, farm plot, sacks, document recreation, handoffs). The coyote truck is added to the common module here for ep. 10.
- **One chart rig** serves scenes 5, 6, 7 and 9: an ink C-price line from 1962 to 2026 on one axis. Scenes only change the visible window and the overlays (the corridor with its two gaps, the crash and the five-year average bars, the dashed floor). Keep the line as one path whose trim is driven per scene.
- **The rail** (S3–S4) has four stops because the price passes through four places. The differential and the peso are effects on the price, not places, so they ride the bar as a tag and a dial.
- **S4 honesty:** the deductions are drawn as equal-width hatched segments marked "?", because no source gives their sizes for Mexico. Only their total is sourced (the farm price against the New York price), and the green remainder is drawn to that.
- **Farmer's share, how it's computed:** SIAP's 2020 national *precio medio rural* for café cereza, ≈ MXN 5.39/kg, × 5–6.5 kg cherry per kg green = MXN 27–35 per kg green; ÷ the 2020 average exchange rate (≈ 21.5 MXN/USD) = $1.25–1.63/kg = $0.57–0.74/lb; against a 2020 C-price average of about $1.11/lb that is ≈ 50–67 %. Applied to 15¢: ≈ 8–10¢. The 2020 ratio is applied to the 2026 cup; replace it with a newer SIAP year and the matching C average at lock.
- **Per-cup maths (S2):** 22 g green = 0.0485 lb; × $3.00 = $0.145. Farm maths (S5): 2 ha × ≈ 0.36 t/ha = ≈ 700 kg = 1,543 lb; × $0.50 = $772; × $3.96 = $6,110. If the C price has moved when the VO is recorded, recompute S2 and keep the 2001 and 2025 figures.
- **White-frame pass:** no backgrounds needed changing; S4's final beat greys the route, tags and dial and clears the sacks before the green remainder; S6 clears the treaty page and the quota bar so the corridor is the one focal element (the bar returns briefly in S7 to spill).
- **Brands:** every recreated document, board and pack is a generic flat recreation with no logos.
