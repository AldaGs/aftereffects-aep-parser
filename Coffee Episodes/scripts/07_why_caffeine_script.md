# Why Does a Plant Make Caffeine?: Script and Storyboard

**Status:** draft v2 (after fact check, visual direction and story edit, see `notes/`).
**Format:** 4:07 (606 words of VO at 155 wpm, plus a 1 s hold per scene and a 2 s end card), 1920×1080, 30 fps, AE 26.5.
**The question:** What is a coffee plant making caffeine for?
**The answer:** Caffeine is a defence chemical, and the plant sets the dose by tissue: enough in seeds and young leaves to stop most insects, a trace in the nectar that makes a pollinating bee remember the flower and come back.
**The surprise:** The same molecule that poisons a caterpillar is, at nectar strength, a memory aid for bees. Lands in scene 7, the turn (≈ 2:10). Then the beetle that does the most damage to coffee lives inside the seed, the most caffeinated part of the plant, and its gut bacteria eat caffeine as food (scene 8).
**Mexico's part:** Mexico is where the defence is tested and where the answer to its failure comes from. Robusta, the high-caffeine species, is about one bag in eight of Mexico's harvest and growing, in the Chiapas and Veracruz lowlands† (scene 6). The broca crossed into Chiapas in 1978†, and Chiapas borers carry caffeine-eating bacteria†. Since caffeine can't stop it, Mexico brought in African wasps that hunt the borer inside the cherry (1988†, scene 9).
**Reused from earlier episodes:** the ep. 1 cherry and its seed cross-section (S2, S8, S9), the ep. 1 leaf (S4, S5; grey on generic plants, Mexico green on Chiapas plants, per the foliage rule), the ep. 1 red and brown species circles and mountain icon (S6), the ep. 1 Mexico map with state outlines (S6, S8). The caffeine molecule and caffeine blue come from ep. 6; the bacteria use ep. 5's ferment magenta.
**New colours:** pest violet `#6C4F9E` (insect pests: the hornworm and the broca), from the locked season palette. Caffeine blue `#3B73B9` was introduced in ep. 6. The bee and the wasps are ink, because they aren't pests.

## Logline

A bee drinks caffeinated nectar from a coffee flower. The plant spends scarce nitrogen on that molecule, and coffee, tea, cacao, guarana and citrus each invented it separately, so it must pay. It pays as a nerve poison for caterpillars and a root poison for rival seedlings, and robusta, which carries the most, grows where pests are thickest. Then the turn: at nectar strength the poison is a memory aid. In Chiapas the story bends twice more: the borer that ruins coffee lives inside the seed and carries bacteria that eat caffeine, and Mexico's answer is a wasp.

## Script and storyboard

### 1. Cold open: caffeinated nectar (0:00–0:15, `S1_Flower`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** White frame. A coffee branch draws on from the left: grey leaves with ink outlines (a generic plant, per the foliage rule) and a cluster of white five-petal flowers. A honeybee, ink outline with a soft shadow, lands on the centre flower.
- **B:** The camera pushes in. A drop of nectar at the flower's base fills caffeine blue, and a small caffeine molecule outline floats up from it with a leader line: CAFFEINE. Four of its atoms, the nitrogens, are ringed in ink: 4 NITROGEN ATOMS PER MOLECULE.
- **C:** The headline types on above: *Coffea arabica*, in Georgia Italic.
- **D:** The headline wipes off and the camera pushes in on the drop until it's a 40 px blue dot at centre. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "A honeybee is drinking from a coffee flower, and the nectar it's drinking contains caffeine. Caffeine is built from nitrogen, which the plant has to pull out of the soil. What is it spending that nitrogen on?"

### 2. Where the caffeine is (0:15–0:32, `S2_Plant`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The blue dot becomes the NECTAR callout on a full coffee plant, drawn flat, as the camera pulls out. Leader lines with dots run from the seed, a young leaf and the nectar to one shared horizontal scale along the bottom, CAFFEINE BY DRY WEIGHT, %, 0 to 3: SEED (ARABICA) ≈ 1 %†, YOUNG LEAF ≈ 1–3 %†, NECTAR: A TRACE, BELOW A BEE'S TASTE THRESHOLD†. Source line: SOURCE: CEJA-NAVARRO ET AL. (2015); ASHIHARA & CROZIER (2001); WRIGHT ET AL. (2013).
- **B:** A brown tick joins the scale: SEED (ROBUSTA) ≈ 2 %†, with ep. 1's brown circle as its marker.
- **C:** A cherry on the plant (ep. 1 cherry) slides out to centre and opens into its cross-section. The camera pushes into one cream seed.
- **D:** Inside the seed, one caffeine molecule outline grows to 360 px, centred. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "Caffeine isn't spread evenly through a coffee plant. An arabica seed is about one percent caffeine, a robusta seed about two. Young leaves are loaded with it. The nectar has only a trace, too little for a bee to taste."

### 3. Built once, invented five times (0:32–1:08, `S3_Origins`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The caffeine molecule's three methyl tabs retract and snap back on in sequence along a short arrow, a quick 4 s build (no step labels), passing through one labelled stop: THEOBROMINE · THE STIMULANT IN CHOCOLATE. Source line: SOURCE: ASHIHARA, SANO & CROZIER, *PHYTOCHEMISTRY* (2008).
- **B:** The molecule shrinks to a 24 px blue dot, which becomes the COFFEE tip of a simplified flowering-plant tree that draws downward and outward, guide grey, with rounded elbows (ep. 1 tree style). Four more tips light along its top edge, far apart on different branches: TEA, CACAO, GUARANA, CITRUS.
- **C:** A dashed "single ancestor" node appears at the root, glowing blue, with a question mark. The 2014 coffee-genome paper slides in as a flat page, credited lower-left (DENOEUD ET AL., *SCIENCE*, 2014†), with a highlighter swipe on "convergent evolution". The root node greys out.
- **D:** The genome page slides off. A short blue segment lights just below each of the five tips, separately, on its own branch. A counter in Chonburi: AT LEAST 5†, with five tally marks on the tree itself as the unit. Source line: SOURCE: HUANG ET AL., *PNAS* (2016).
- **E:** The four nitrogen rings from S1 flash once on the coffee tip's molecule icon. The camera pushes into the COFFEE tip, which unfolds into the ep. 1 leaf (grey), 600 px. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "The plant builds caffeine in four steps, bolting small carbon groups onto a cousin of DNA's building blocks. One step short of caffeine, it makes theobromine, the stimulant in chocolate. Tea makes caffeine too, and so do cacao, guarana and some citrus flowers. The coffee genome, published in 2014, showed that coffee's caffeine genes are its own, not inherited from an ancestor it shares with tea. A 2016 study counted at least five separate inventions. Evolution kept arriving at the same molecule, though it costs scarce nitrogen, which suggests it pays."

### 4. A poison for insects (1:08–1:35, `S4_Nerve`, the mechanism)
**Storyboard.** The leaf stays in the left half; the right half holds one large nerve cell. No numbered rail: the mechanism is a loop, and the camera follows it round.
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* A tobacco hornworm, pest violet, crawls onto the leaf's edge and starts eating: a bite-shaped notch grows in the leaf outline. A recreated journal page slides in, credited lower-left (NATHANSON, *SCIENCE*, 1984†), with a highlighter swipe on "naturally occurring pesticides".
- **B:** The journal page slides off. The nerve cell (right half). A signal arrives as an ink pulse, and leaves a messenger behind: small ink dots (cAMP) inside the cell. An enzyme, an ink pac-man shape, eats the dots one by one, and the cell's outline settles: label THE CELL GOES QUIET.
- **C:** A caffeine molecule (blue) drifts in and wedges into the enzyme's mouth. The next signal arrives; the messenger dots pile up uneaten, and the cell outline flashes over and over: label CAFFEINE JAMS THE OFF SWITCH.
- **D:** Back on the leaf, the notch stops growing. The caterpillar stiffens and rolls off.
- **E:** The leaf detaches and falls onto a soil line at y = 880. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "In 1984 a Harvard researcher, James Nathanson, fed caffeine to tobacco hornworm caterpillars. At doses like those in leaves, they stopped eating, and higher doses killed them. Inside an insect's nerve cell, every signal leaves a messenger behind. An enzyme clears it away, and the cell goes quiet. Caffeine sits in that enzyme's mouth. The messenger piles up, the cell keeps firing, and the caterpillar stops eating."

### 7. The dose (2:08–2:46, `S7_Dose`, the turn)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The flower holds; a bee lands on it.
- **B:** A lab version of the scene builds beside it: two bees in harnesses (ink), each in front of a scented drop. One gets SUGAR, one gets SUGAR + NECTAR-LEVEL CAFFEINE (the drop is caffeine blue). A recreated *Science* page slides in, credited lower-left (WRIGHT ET AL., 2013).
- **C:** A bar chart builds on a shared axis, BEES THAT REMEMBERED THE SCENT, with paired bars at two times: 24 H, where the blue bar is three times the grey one†, and 72 H, where it's twice†. Source line: SOURCE: WRIGHT ET AL., *SCIENCE* 339:1202 (2013).
- **D:** The chart slides up and a horizontal **dose scale** draws across the bottom (y = 900), log-spaced: CAFFEINE DOSE, LOW → HIGH. The bee drops onto the low end: MEMORY AID. The hornworm from S4 drops onto the high end: POISON. Highlighter swipe on THE DOSE DECIDES.
- **E:** The camera pushes in on the hornworm, which curls into a 16 px pest-violet dot. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "So caffeine is a poison. But this bee isn't being poisoned. In 2013 a team led by Geraldine Wright gave honeybees sugar water, some of it laced with caffeine at nectar strength. A day later, the caffeinated bees were three times as likely to remember the flower's scent. After three days they were still twice as likely. At this dose, caffeine sharpens memory, through the same kind of receptor it blocks in your brain. A bee that remembers comes back, carrying pollen. The molecule doesn't change. The dose decides whether it's a weapon or a reward."

### 8. La broca (2:46–3:20, `S8_Broca`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The violet dot is the arrowhead of an arrow drawn in from Guatemala, landing on the Mexico map: CACAHOATÁN, CHIAPAS, 1978†. Source line: SOURCE: SENASICA; DOF QUARANTINE DECREE, 1978. The violet then spreads along the coffee belt as overlapping ellipses scaling up under a coffee-belt track matte, over several seconds.
- **B:** The map wipes away to the ep. 1 cherry at centre, 220 px, beside a sesame seed drawn to the same scale. A borer beetle, pest violet, sits between them: ≈ 2 MM† next to SESAME ≈ 3 MM. It bores into the cherry: a small entry hole at the tip, then the cherry opens into its cross-section and a tunnel draws through one seed, the seed outlined caffeine blue.
- **C:** The camera pushes into the beetle's gut, a simple cut-away tube. Rod-shaped bacteria in ferment magenta cluster there. Blue caffeine dots flow in from the left and disappear into the bacteria. Label: *Pseudomonas fulva* AND OTHERS LIVE ON CAFFEINE†. The gut cut-away goes guide grey. A world-map strip slides up with seven dots where the borers were sampled: Kenya, Indonesia, India, Puerto Rico, Hawaii, Guatemala and Chiapas, with a highlighter swipe on Chiapas. Source line: SOURCE: CEJA-NAVARRO ET AL., *NAT. COMMUN.* (2015).
- **D:** The map strip slides off and the gut returns to colour. An antibiotic pill outline drops into the gut. The bacteria grey out, and the blue dots pile up unbroken: NO BACTERIA, NO DIGESTION. Hold for 2 s.
- **E:** The camera pulls back out to the whole cherry, closed, 220 px, centred, with the borer's hole at its tip. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "But the insect that does the most damage to coffee eats caffeine anyway. The coffee berry borer, la broca, crossed from Guatemala into Cacahoatán, Chiapas, in 1978. It's a beetle smaller than a sesame seed, and it spends its life inside the coffee seed, the most caffeinated part of the plant. In 2015 researchers sampled borers from seven coffee regions, Chiapas among them. Their guts carried bacteria that live on caffeine. Kill the bacteria with antibiotics and the beetles couldn't break caffeine down at all."

- **B:** The cherry opens into its cross-section. The wasp follows the borer's tunnel into the seed and lays small ink eggs on the borer's larvae. The borer young grey out.
- **C:** Of the two names, *Prorops nasuta* fades to guide grey and *Cephalonomia stephanoderis* gets a highlighter swipe: ESTABLISHED†.
- **D:** The cherry closes and slides up onto a Chiapas coffee branch (Mexico-green leaves) beside a flower that is opening. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "Mexico's answer was an animal caffeine can't stop. In 1988, researchers brought two species of tiny African wasp to Chiapas. They crawl into the cherry after the borer and lay their eggs on its young. One of the two settled in. The plant's chemistry keeps out the insects that eat everything. The one that got past it is now hunted by an insect that doesn't eat coffee at all."

### 10. Close (3:47–4:07, `S10_Close`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The branch holds; the flower is open and the bee lands on it, beside the cherry.
- **B:** Two leader lines draw from one caffeine-blue dot: one to the seed, labelled DEFENCE, and one to the nectar, labelled REWARD. The S7 dose scale flashes along the bottom once, with the bee at the low end and the borer added beside the hornworm at the high end.

**VO:** "Caffeine is the plant's defence, and the plant chooses the dose: enough in the seed to stop most insects, a trace in the nectar to bring the bee back. The broca evolved its way around the dose. The bee never had to."

## Notes for the build

- **One precomp per scene**, butt-cut on `Why_Caffeine`. Match-cut handoff values (table above) are defined once as constants in the generator.
- **The mechanism is S4's nerve cell**, built as a loop (signal → messenger → enzyme clears it → quiet), then the same loop jammed. Hold beat C until the cell has flashed at least three times.
- **The dose scale (S7)** is the episode's key image. It returns in S10, so build it as its own precomp.
- **S8's violet stain:** overlapping ellipses scaling under a coffee-belt track matte (per visual direction), not a uniform wipe.
- **Foliage:** grey with ink outlines on the generic plant (S1–S5); Mexico green only on the Chiapas plants (S6 D, S9 D, S10).
- **Molecules** come from the shared kit (ep. 6): ink ball-and-stick, methyl tabs in caffeine blue only when they're the subject.
- **Loops** (bee wings, the flashing cell, rain dots) are expressions, not keyframes.
- **The S3 tree is schematic.** The five tips sit far apart so "separate inventions" reads.
- **White-frame pass:** the S5 soil cross-section is white below an ink line, not a grey band; S6's mountain is an outline; S3, S4 and S8 clear their document pages and map strip before the next element arrives, so one thing is in focus at a time.
