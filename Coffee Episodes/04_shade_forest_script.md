# The Shade Forest in Your Cup: Script and Storyboard

**Status:** draft v2 (revised against `notes/fact_check.md`, `notes/visual_direction.md` and `notes/story_edit.md`).
**Format:** 4:41 (687 words of VO ≈ 4:26 at 155 wpm, plus about 15 s of holds), 1920×1080, 30 fps, AE 26.5.
**The question:** Why do some coffee farms look like jungle from the air?
**The answer:** Arabica began as a forest shrub. On shade farms the trees still do work a grower would otherwise have to pay for: they flatten the daily temperature swing, pull nitrogen out of the air and cover the soil with leaf litter. On an organic farm, that work can't be bought at all. The price is fewer kilos of coffee per hectare.
**The surprise:** Shade has a bill. It cost about 18 % of the yield in one trial, and it pushes leaf rust both ways at once: wetter leaves help the fungus, a lighter crop helps the plant. Lands in scenes 8 and 9 (3:16).
**Mexico's part:** The way scientists classify shade farms was built on Mexican farms (Moguel & Toledo 1999), the microclimate evidence comes from the Soconusco, and Mexico's organic sector (third by certified area in 2021) depends on shade. An organic farm can't buy synthetic nitrogen, so what the chalahuite tree fixes is nitrogen nobody has to pay for. In Mexico, the forest is part of the farm's input bill, on the credit side.
**Reused from earlier episodes:** the world map and the Ethiopian highlands dot (ep. 1, S2/S6), the Mexico map with state outlines (ep. 1, S7), the leaf with its rust-spot matte (ep. 1, S9) and ep. 2's water film on the leaf (ep. 2, S3 step 1), the red cherry (ep. 1, S1), and the generic bag outline and document recreation from `coffee_common.py`.
**New colours:** canopy sage `#8FA88A` (shade trees and leaf litter), from the locked season palette. Sun-grown, no-canopy states are guide grey. Mexican coffee shrubs have Mexico-green leaves; the Ethiopian understorey has grey leaves with an ink outline.

## Logline

From the air, a coffee farm in Chiapas is only treetops. The episode goes back to arabica's start as a forest shrub. It trucks along one continuous slice of land, Moguel & Toledo's five kinds of Mexican coffee farm from forest to full sun, and then drops into a single shade farm. There it watches the canopy level out the day's heat and the chalahuite's roots pull nitrogen from the air. The bird on a bag of this coffee checks the trees, not the work, and its migrants lead to Mexico's organic farms, which rely on that free nitrogen. Past the middle the bill arrives. Shade costs coffee, and against leaf rust it cuts both ways. The close rises back to the treetops, which now read as a ledger.

## Script and storyboard

Timecodes are for the master comp.

### 1. Treetops (0:00–0:16, `S1_Aerial`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** White frame. A top-down aerial draws on: a dense cluster of about forty sage crown discs, overlapping with no rows between them, in a patch about 900 × 450 px running up to a thin ink ridge line. The white around the patch stays empty. A small label: CHIAPAS, A COFFEE FARM.
- **B:** The camera pushes down through one crown. The view turns into a side-view cross-section and a single coffee shrub with Mexico-green leaves and red cherries appears under the crown, centred. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "From the air, this coffee farm in Chiapas is all treetops, right up to the ridge. The coffee is underneath, in the shade, where nobody flying over can see it. Why would anyone grow coffee in a forest?"

### 2. A forest plant (0:16–0:37, `S2_Understory`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The shrub sits under its crown in an Ethiopian highland forest. Its leaves turn grey with an ink outline, and two more sage crowns draw in beside its own, high above it, on one grey ground line. A height ruler on the left shows the trees at several times the shrub's height. A small inset of the ep. 1 world map pulls out from a red dot in the Ethiopian highlands: WILD ARABICA, UNDER THE FOREST CANOPY.
- **B:** A thin timeline draws along the bottom, with the forest at its left end and a grey sun icon arriving late: SUN COFFEE, 1970s ON†. Source line: `SOURCE: PERFECTO ET AL. 1996`. *(Match cut out on the cross-section.)*

**VO:** "Part of the answer is where arabica comes from. In the highland forests of Ethiopia it grows wild as an understory shrub, below trees several times its height. It evolved in the shade. Growing it in open sun is the newer idea, and most sun farms date from the nineteen seventies on."

### 3. One slope, five farms (0:37–1:06, `S3_Transect`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The camera pulls back. The forest drawing continues rightwards as one long side-view transect across a single slope. The canopy thins continuously from left to right, and at the right end there is no canopy at all, only grey sun rays on denser rows of shrubs. The ground is one grey line and the crowns are flat sage shapes on white. A document recreation sits lower-left: Moguel & Toledo's five-system diagram from *Conservation Biology* (1999), redrawn flat, with a highlighter swipe on its title. Credit: `MOGUEL & TOLEDO 1999`. It slides off before the camera starts to truck.
- **B:** The camera trucks slowly along the transect. Labels drop onto the ground line at five points as it passes, each at the place where the drawing changes. At RUSTIC the native forest is intact. At TRADITIONAL POLYCULTURE, fruit trees (an orange and a banana) appear among the native crowns. At COMMERCIAL POLYCULTURE, the native crowns are gone and timber and fruit trees take their place. At SHADED MONOCULTURE, one crown repeats, labelled *Inga*. At SUN, the crowns are gone. No panels and no frames: the labels sit on the one drawing.
- **C:** Under the transect, a single bar draws from the left with a soft right edge that fades out past the first two labels: ≈ 2/3 OF MEXICO'S COFFEE LAND†. Source line: `SOURCE: MOGUEL & TOLEDO 1999`.
- **D:** The camera stops at the SHADED MONOCULTURE point and pushes into the *Inga* crown. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "In 1999 two Mexican ecologists, Patricia Moguel and Víctor Toledo, sorted Mexico's coffee farms by how much forest is left in them. They found five kinds, and each one strips out a little more forest than the last, from rustic farms, where coffee grows straight under the native trees, to sun coffee, which keeps none. When they looked, roughly two thirds of Mexico's coffee land was still at the forest end."

### 4. The hottest hours (1:06–1:38, `S4_HotHours`, mechanism step 1)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The crown fills the top of a large Chiapas farm cross-section: sage canopy, coffee shrubs with Mexico-green leaves, a row of small sage leaf shapes for litter on one ink ground line, and roots as grey lines below it on white (no filled soil band). It is one precomp with one camera for scenes 4–6. Sun rays hit the canopy, most stop there, and a few reach the shrubs.
- **B:** An overlay builds in the lower third over the shrubs. A small map pin reads SOCONUSCO, CHIAPAS, and three weather-station icons with shade bars sit beside it: 10–30 %, 35–65 %, 60–80 % SHADE. A 24-hour chart draws three temperature traces on a shared axis. Their averages sit on one dashed line labelled AVERAGE: CLOSE. The low-shade trace swings widest and the high-shade trace stays near the dashed line. The y-axis has no degree values, only the spread. Source line: `SOURCE: LIN 2007, AGRICULTURAL AND FOREST METEOROLOGY`.
- **C:** The overlay clears. The camera holds on canopy and shrubs. *(Camera handoff out.)*

**VO:** "On a shaded farm, the trees are doing real work. In the Soconusco, in southern Chiapas, the ecologist Brenda Lin set up weather stations on three coffee farms, under light, medium and heavy shade. Over a season their averages came out close. What changed was the swing: with less shade, the temperature and humidity lurched further every day. Under heavy shade the day stayed level. A coffee plant copes with an average. It's the hottest hours that hurt it."

### 5. Free nitrogen (1:38–2:05, `S5_Nitrogen`, mechanism step 2)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Camera handoff in.)* The camera tilts down past the shrubs to the roots of one *Inga* tree. Small root nodules pop on. Arrows draw from the air in the soil into the nodules: N₂ → NITROGEN THE TREE CAN USE. A label: CHALAHUITE (*Inga* spp.) ≈ 35–40 kg N / HECTARE / YEAR†. Beside it, two flat fertiliser sacks stack up as a visual unit: ≈ 2 SACKS OF UREA†. Source line: `SOURCE: INSTITUTO DE ECOLOGÍA, XALAPA`.
- **B:** Leaves drift down from above the frame and settle into the sage litter band over the soil. Water-cyan rain lines hit the band instead of bare soil. The camera holds on litter and soil. *(Camera handoff out.)*

**VO:** "Down at the roots, a common shade tree in Veracruz does a second job. Chalahuite, a kind of Inga, is a legume, so bacteria in its roots pull nitrogen out of the air: roughly thirty-five to forty kilos per hectare each year, about what's in two sacks of urea fertiliser. Its leaves fall and cover the ground, so the rain lands on litter instead of bare soil."

### 6. What the bird checks (2:05–2:47, `S6_BirdCheck`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Camera handoff in.)* The camera tilts back up through the shrubs to the canopy. A generic coffee-bag outline slides in from the right edge with a small bird stamp on it (a generic silhouette, not the Smithsonian seal). The certification conditions draw onto the cross-section itself, not as a list, one at a time, each going guide grey as the next arrives: a height ruler on the canopy (≥ 12 m), a dashed outline of the shaded area on the ground line (≥ 40 % COVER), small numbered tags on eleven different crowns (≥ 11 TREE SPECIES†), and a stamp on the soil (ORGANIC FIRST). Source line: `SOURCE: SMITHSONIAN BIRD FRIENDLY NORMS`.
- **B:** The bag slides off. About thirty small ink bird silhouettes pop onto the branches while a counter runs to 150+ SPECIES†; the number carries the count, not the birds (pre-placed groups with staggered opacity; the migrants are a second layer drawn as outlines). Source line: `SOURCE: SMITHSONIAN MIGRATORY BIRD CENTER`.
- **C:** A split line wipes across, and the right half shows the same cross-section without trees. Fewer birds stand on it, and the outline birds that went missing first are the canopy ones. Label: SUN FARMS: FEWER BIRDS, FOREST SPECIES FIRST TO GO†. Source line: `SOURCE: GREENBERG ET AL. 1997; PHILPOTT ET AL. 2008`.
- **D:** One migrant outline lifts off. The camera pulls out from the farm onto the ep. 1 world map. The migrant's ink route draws north to the Great Lakes, then back south, and lands on the same Chiapas farm dot. *(Match cut out on the dot.)*

**VO:** "None of that is what the bird on the bag checks. To earn the Smithsonian's Bird Friendly seal, a farm needs a canopy at least twelve metres tall over forty percent of its ground, with eleven or more kinds of tree, on a farm that's already organic. Researchers have counted more than a hundred and fifty bird species on shaded farms in Chiapas. On sun farms, studies found fewer birds, and the forest species were the first to go. Many of the shade birds are migrants that nest as far north as the Great Lakes and spend the winter on farms like this one."

### 7. Nitrogen nobody buys (2:47–3:16, `S7_Organic`)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The camera pushes in from the world map to the ep. 1 Mexico map around the farm dot. Chiapas and Oaxaca fill Mexico green, and small sage tree icons scatter across their coffee zones.
- **B:** The map slides left. A horizontal bar chart builds on the right: CERTIFIED ORGANIC COFFEE LAND, 2021: Ethiopia ≈ 183,000 ha, Peru ≈ 169,000 ha, Mexico ≈ 83,000 ha. Beside Mexico's bar sits a small outline of Mexico City at the same area scale: MEXICO'S ORGANIC COFFEE LAND ≈ HALF THE AREA OF MEXICO CITY†. Source line: `SOURCE: FIBL 2021 DATA, VIA AGRARIA.PE`.
- **C:** A synthetic-fertiliser sack slides towards the Mexico bar and is crossed out. An arrow from the chalahuite nodules (a small inset from scene 5) runs into the bar, and a second small arrow comes from a heap of coffee pulp labelled COMPOST. A stack of coffee sacks grows beside the bar. *(Match cut out on the sack stack.)*

**VO:** "That last condition is where Mexico comes in. In 2021, Mexico had the third-largest area of certified organic coffee in the world, after Ethiopia and Peru, most of it on small farms in Chiapas and Oaxaca. An organic farm isn't allowed synthetic nitrogen. On these farms, what the chalahuite pulls out of the air is nitrogen nobody has to buy, and with composted coffee pulp it's most of what the coffee gets."

### 8. The price of shade (3:16–3:39, `S8_Yield`, the turn)
**Storyboard**
- **A:** *(Match cut in.)* The sack stack becomes the left bar of a two-bar chart: FULL SUN at 100. Beside it, ≈ 45 % SHADE at 82. - **B:** A tag slides onto the shade bar: BIGGER BEANS, BETTER CUP. A second tag hangs on the empty gap: COFFEE IS SOLD BY THE KILO. The camera pushes in on the shade bar's top edge. *(Match cut out.)*

**VO:** "But shade has a price. In a trial in Costa Rica, coffee under about half shade gave eighteen percent less than coffee in full sun. Its beans were bigger and it tasted better, but a farmer paid by the kilo had fewer kilos to sell. That trade is one reason so many farms cut their trees."

### 9. Shade and roya (3:39–4:06, `S9_Roya`)

Across Chiapas, though, more layers of trees meant less rust."

## Notes for the build

- **One cross-section precomp for scenes 4–6, 9's leaf landing and 10.** Its layers are named by stratum (canopy, coffee, litter, soil, roots). A single camera null drives scenes 4–6. Every 4→5 and 5→6 cut is a camera handoff: the generator writes the camera's end transform for scene N as the start transform of scene N+1, so the cut is invisible. Overlays (S4's chart) sit above the camera rig and clear before any tilt.
- **The transect (S3)** is one long drawing built in Python as groups inside a few shape layers, with no panels. Canopy density is a function of x, so the forest thins continuously. The camera trucks along it, and the five labels are pinned to the ground line at the x where the drawing changes. This replaces the v1 row of five panels, which read as cards.
- **Temperature traces (S4)** are three paths from one sine expression with different amplitudes (`avg + amp * Math.sin(...)`), so the averages match exactly and only the swing changes. They show no hotter-afternoon or cooler-night asymmetry until the full Lin paper is read.
- **Birds (S6)** are pre-placed silhouette groups generated in Python, with staggered opacity. The migrant outlines are a second layer, because a repeater can't vary style per copy. Keep the silhouette generic, and don't reproduce the Smithsonian seal.
- **Foliage:** Mexican shrubs have Mexico-green leaves. Ethiopian shrubs have grey leaves with an ink outline. The colour change at 1→2 happens after the cut frame.
- **Colours:** canopy sage only for trees and litter. Mexico green only on Mexican states and Mexican leaves. Roya orange only on rust spots. Water cyan for the rain and the leaf's water film. Sun-grown states are guide grey.
- **White-frame pass:** the S1/S11 aerial is a crown patch on white, not a frame-filling carpet; S2's forest is three crowns (cuts 2→3 and 3→4 updated); the cross-section's soil is a ground line with grey roots, not a filled band; S3's document card leaves before the truck; S6's conditions arrive one at a time and the 150+ birds are about thirty silhouettes and a counter.
- **Fonts:** Chonburi-Regular, Georgia-Italic, ArialMT and Arial-BoldMT.

## Facts to check (†)

- **Arabica grows wild as an understory shrub in Ethiopian highland forests.** Standard botany. Confirm the wording with Davis et al. 2012 (Kew).
- **Most sun farms date from the 1970s on.** Consistent with Perfecto et al. 1996, *BioScience* 46: 598–608 (fact check).
- **Moguel & Toledo 1999: five systems, 60–70 % of Mexico's coffee area under traditional management.** *Conservation Biology* 13: 11–21 (confirmed). Which systems count as "traditional" is still to be checked. The S3 bar uses a soft edge past the first two labels until it is.
- **Lin 2007: three Soconusco farms at 10–30 %, 35–65 % and 60–80 % shade. Seasonal means differed little, but temperature and humidity fluctuations grew as shade fell.** *Agric. For. Meteorol.* 144: 85–94 (abstract only). The v2 VO says only that the swing grew, which the fact check rated safe. "Hotter afternoons" is removed.
- **"It's the hottest hours that hurt it."** Arabica photosynthesis falls off above about 24 °C (DaMatta & Ramalho 2006). Confirm.
- **Chalahuite ≈ 35–40 kg N/ha/year.** Instituto de Ecología (Xalapa) seed manual, citing Nair. Not checked by the fact check. Keep "roughly".
- **≈ 2 sacks of urea.** Arithmetic: 40 kg N ÷ 0.46 ≈ 87 kg ≈ two 50 kg sacks.
- **Bird Friendly: canopy ≥ 12 m, ≥ 40 % cover after pruning, organic required (confirmed); ≥ 11 tree species.** SMBC norms. The backbone species may be up to 60 % of shade trees, and the other 40 % must include at least 10 species, so at least 11 in all. Check whether the revised standard has changed these.
- **More than 150 bird species on Chiapas shade farms.** SMBC ("over 150" in traditionally managed plantations in eastern Chiapas). The v1 figure of 180 is dropped as unverified. Confirm with Greenberg, Bichier & Sterling 1997, *Biotropica* 29: 501–514.
- **Sun farms: fewer birds, forest species first to go.** Greenberg et al. 1997 (Guatemala: shade had ≈ 15 % more species and ≈ 30 % more birds) and Philpott et al. 2008, *Conservation Biology* 22: 1093–1105 (largest losses on sun farms). This replaces v1's "most of those species gone".
- **Migrants nesting as far north as the Great Lakes winter on these farms.** General SMBC material on Neotropical migrants in shade coffee. Confirm with a named species (e.g. a warbler with a Great Lakes breeding range and Chiapas wintering records).
- **Organic coffee land, 2021: Ethiopia ≈ 183,000 ha, Peru ≈ 169,000 ha, Mexico ≈ 83,000 ha (third).** FiBL data via agraria.pe (confirmed for 2021 only). The year is on screen. Update from the FiBL statistics portal if newer data shows the same order.
- **≈ half the area of Mexico City.** Arithmetic: 83,000 ha against CDMX ≈ 148,500 ha.
- **"Most of it on small farms in Chiapas and Oaxaca."** Confirm with SADER/SENASICA organic registry data or CERTIMEX.
- **Chalahuite nitrogen plus composted pulp is "most of what the coffee gets" on these farms.** Confirm against a Mexican organic nutrient budget (ECOSUR or CERTIMEX). If it isn't supported, change it to "a large share of".
- **Vaast et al. 2006: ≈ 45 % shade, −18 % yield, larger beans, better beverage.** Confirmed (HAL abstract; Costa Rica central valley; Costa Rica 95).
- **"That trade is one reason so many farms cut their trees."** Interpretation, worded as the fact check proposed.
- **Rust needs a wet leaf (ep. 2, step 1). At equal fruit load, rust was higher under shade, and shade lowers rust mainly by reducing the fruit load.** López-Bravo, Virginio Filho & Avelino 2012, *Crop Protection* 38: 21–29. Not checked by the fact check.
- **Shaded farms were hit when the rust outbreak reached Chiapas.** Confirm with Avelino et al. 2015, *Food Security* 7: 303–321.
- **Chiapas: more strata of shade, less rust.** Soto-Pinto, Perfecto & Caballero-Nieto 2002, *Agroforestry Systems* 55: 37–45 (confirmed).
- **The Bird Friendly seal is "the part the buyer pays for"**, i.e. it carries a price premium. Confirm with SMBC or importer data on Bird Friendly premiums. If none can be shown, change it to "the part the buyer can see."
