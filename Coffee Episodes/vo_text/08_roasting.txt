In some Mexican roasters, sugar goes into the drum with the coffee and comes out as a black glaze. This other Mexican bag was roasted with no sugar at all, and its label promises caramel too. Where does that caramel come from?

A green coffee seed smells of grass and is too hard to grind. Half of it is cell wall. What the roast works on is smaller: about seven percent sugar, six percent chlorogenic acids, a little free amino acid, and, on top of all that, about eleven percent water.

A roast takes about ten to fifteen minutes, and roasters steer it by the beans' temperature, which climbs along a curve. First the water has to go. Up to about a hundred and sixty degrees, the seed dries and turns yellow, and the room smells of hay, then of bread.

Then the browning starts. It's the Maillard reaction, the same one that browns toast. A sugar meets an amino acid, and they lock together. The pair rearranges, and more heat shatters it into small, reactive fragments. Those fragments tear pieces off other amino acids, and the pieces fly off as smells: malt, cooked potato. That's called the Strecker degradation. What's left pairs up into pyrazines, the smell of roasted nuts, and the rest chains together into brown pigments called melanoidins. Chemists have counted about a thousand aroma compounds in roasted coffee. A few dozen carry most of the smell.

At around a hundred and ninety-six degrees, the beans start to pop. This is first crack. The water still inside has turned to steam, and the reactions have been making carbon dioxide. Both are trapped in cells whose walls have gone brittle. The pressure builds until the walls give, with a crack you can hear across the room. The bean swells to half again its size.

After first crack, the roaster is choosing how the coffee will taste. The chlorogenic acids are breaking apart: about two thirds are gone at a medium roast, and closer to nine in ten a shade darker. Their pieces turn into bitter compounds, and harsher ones the further you go. Stop early and the coffee tastes more of where it was grown. Keep going and it tastes more of the roast.

Push on to about two hundred and twenty-five degrees and you reach second crack, a softer crackle, as the cell walls themselves break down and oil comes to the surface. By then the bean has lost about a sixth of its weight, most of it water, the rest gas and smell.

Here is what happened to the sugar. By a medium roast, almost all of it is gone. Some went into the brown. The rest broke down on its own, which is caramelisation, and left behind molecules that smell of caramel. So the caramel on that bag is real, but it's a smell. The sugar that made it has been spent.

Mexico has an official standard for coffee roasted with caramelised sugar. Near the end of the roast, sugar goes into the drum and melts onto the beans as a black glaze. It's caramelisation on the outside of the bean instead of the inside. When Mexico's consumer agency tested thirty-three ground coffees in 2025, four were sugar blends. Mexico's specialty roasters do the opposite. They stop soon after first crack and let the bean make its own caramel.

Roasting is a seed spending its sugar, on colour and on smell, until almost none is left. The drum on the left puts sugar back on the outside. The bag on the right got its caramel by using the sugar up.
