This bag of decaf was grown in Colombia. The small print says it lost its caffeine in Córdoba, Veracruz, about three thousand kilometres away. Why would a Colombian bean travel that far to lose its caffeine, and how do you take it out without taking the flavour too?

Caffeine is about one percent of a green arabica bean. Everything else is what you'll taste once it's roasted. In the US, the standard for decaf is that at least ninety-seven percent of that one percent goes. A decaf cup still has a few milligrams. A regular one has about a hundred.

The first to sell decaf was a Bremen coffee merchant, Ludwig Roselius. By 1905 he had a patent: steam the beans, then rinse them in benzene, which we now know causes cancer. By 1970 safer solvents had taken over. Methylene chloride boils off at forty degrees, long before the roaster gets hot. The same year, a German chemist patented carbon dioxide, squeezed until it behaves like a liquid. The obvious solvent was the hardest to make work: water.

Caffeine dissolves in water easily. The trouble is that almost everything else in a bean does too. Soak green coffee in plain water and it comes out with no caffeine and very little taste.

The fix is to keep the flavour from leaving at all. First the green beans are steamed, so they open. Then a first batch soaks until the water holds everything that dissolves out of coffee, caffeine included, and that water runs through a filter that catches the caffeine. Fresh beans go into what's left. Molecules drift from where there are more of them to where there are fewer. The sugars and acids are already as concentrated in the water as in the bean, so they stay put. Caffeine is the one thing the water lacks, so caffeine is the one thing that leaves. The water goes back through the filter and round again, until the beans are about ninety-nine point nine percent caffeine-free. Then they're dried back to the moisture they started with.

This is how Swiss Water does it, at a plant near Vancouver that has run since 1988. Only a handful of plants in the world take caffeine out with water alone, and one of them is in Córdoba, below Pico de Orizaba, Mexico's highest mountain. Colombia has its own decaf plant, but it uses a solvent made from sugarcane. A roaster who wants water and nothing else has to send the beans abroad. The bags make a lot of the mountain water. What's dissolved in it matters more. The company in Córdoba, Descamex, brews a separate extract for each origin, so a Colombian bean soaks in a bath that already tastes Colombian.

The caffeine isn't thrown away. Filtered out of the water, it dries into white crystals that are sold to soft-drink and energy-drink makers.

Decaf, in the end, is a bath with everything in it except caffeine. The bean keeps what the water already has and lets go of the one thing it doesn't. In Córdoba, that bath is made from coffee like the bean in it.
