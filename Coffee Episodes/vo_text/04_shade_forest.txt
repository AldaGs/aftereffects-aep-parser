From the air, this coffee farm in Chiapas is all treetops, right up to the ridge. The coffee is underneath, in the shade, where nobody flying over can see it. Why would anyone grow coffee in a forest?

Part of the answer is where arabica comes from. In the highland forests of Ethiopia it grows wild as an understory shrub, below trees several times its height. It evolved in the shade. Growing it in open sun is the newer idea, and most sun farms date from the nineteen seventies on.

In 1999 two Mexican ecologists, Patricia Moguel and Víctor Toledo, sorted Mexico's coffee farms by how much forest is left in them. They found five kinds, and each one strips out a little more forest than the last, from rustic farms, where coffee grows straight under the native trees, to sun coffee, which keeps none. When they looked, roughly two thirds of Mexico's coffee land was still at the forest end.

On a shaded farm, the trees are doing real work. In the Soconusco, in southern Chiapas, the ecologist Brenda Lin set up weather stations on three coffee farms, under light, medium and heavy shade. Over a season their averages came out close. What changed was the swing: with less shade, the temperature and humidity lurched further every day. Under heavy shade the day stayed level. A coffee plant copes with an average. It's the hottest hours that hurt it.

Down at the roots, a common shade tree in Veracruz does a second job. Chalahuite, a kind of Inga, is a legume, so bacteria in its roots pull nitrogen out of the air: roughly thirty-five to forty kilos per hectare each year, about what's in two sacks of urea fertiliser. Its leaves fall and cover the ground, so the rain lands on litter instead of bare soil.

None of that is what the bird on the bag checks. To earn the Smithsonian's Bird Friendly seal, a farm needs a canopy at least twelve metres tall over forty percent of its ground, with eleven or more kinds of tree, on a farm that's already organic. Researchers have counted more than a hundred and fifty bird species on shaded farms in Chiapas. On sun farms, studies found fewer birds, and the forest species were the first to go. Many of the shade birds are migrants that nest as far north as the Great Lakes and spend the winter on farms like this one.

That last condition is where Mexico comes in. In 2021, Mexico had the third-largest area of certified organic coffee in the world, after Ethiopia and Peru, most of it on small farms in Chiapas and Oaxaca. An organic farm isn't allowed synthetic nitrogen. On these farms, what the chalahuite pulls out of the air is nitrogen nobody has to buy, and with composted coffee pulp it's most of what the coffee gets.

But shade has a price. In a trial in Costa Rica, coffee under about half shade gave eighteen percent less than coffee in full sun. Its beans were bigger and it tasted better, but a farmer paid by the kilo had fewer kilos to sell. That trade is one reason so many farms cut their trees.

The trees don't settle the fight with roya, either. A rust spore needs a wet leaf, and leaves under trees stay wet for longer. But a shaded bush also carries fewer cherries, and a lighter crop leaves the plant better able to fight back. When the outbreak from episode two reached Chiapas, shaded farms were hit too. Across Chiapas, though, more layers of trees meant less rust.

Some farms look like jungle because arabica was a forest plant first, and in Chiapas and Veracruz the forest still does work a farmer would otherwise have to buy, and on an organic farm can't. The bill is paid in kilos. The bird on the bag is the part the buyer pays for.

From the air it still looks like jungle. Every one of those treetops is doing a job for the coffee underneath, and costing it a little coffee too.
