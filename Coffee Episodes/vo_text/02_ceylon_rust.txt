This tin says Ceylon, the old name for Sri Lanka, and it holds tea. A hundred and fifty years ago, a ship from Ceylon would have carried coffee. A fungus killed the island's coffee. The same fungus reached Mexico, and Mexico still grows coffee. Why did Ceylon give up when Mexico didn't?

In 1870, Ceylon shipped about a hundred and eighteen million pounds of coffee, a little under a quarter of what all of Mexico grows today. It came from an island smaller than the state of Chiapas. Before coffee, the island was known for its cinnamon. British planters had cleared the highland forest and filled it with arabica, row after row.

In 1869, a planter noticed orange dust on the underside of his leaves. It was a fungus, Hemileia vastatrix. A spore, about a third the width of a hair, lands under a leaf, and if the leaf stays wet for a day or two, it sprouts. It slips in through a stoma, one of the pores the leaf breathes through, and feeds inside for about two weeks, out of sight. Then it breaks out as an orange spot that sends hundreds of thousands of new spores onto the wind. The leaf falls. A bare tree drains its own wood to ripen its cherries, and the next year it has less to give.

On Ceylon, the spores found a field of near-copies: hundreds of thousands of acres of trees from the narrow family we met in the last episode. A defence that fails on one tree fails on all of them. The monsoon carried spores from estate to estate, and within about five years the rust was in every coffee district.

London sent a young botanist, Harry Marshall Ward, in 1880. He worked out how the fungus spread, but he had nothing that could stop it. By 1886, exports had fallen by about eighty percent. By 1890, planters had abandoned about nine-tenths of their coffee land.

One planter had been trying something else. In 1867, a Scot named James Taylor planted tea on nineteen acres at Loolecondera. As the coffee died, his neighbours copied him. Tea land grew from almost nothing in the mid-1870s to about four hundred thousand acres by 1900. In 1890, a Glasgow grocer, Thomas Lipton, bought estates in the hills and sold the tea in Britain as coming straight from the garden to the teapot.

But the fungus didn't pick tea. The owners did, and they could afford to. Switching meant ripping out the coffee and waiting about three years for a first harvest, with nothing to sell in between. Every estate needed a factory to wither, roll, oxidise and dry the leaf. That took London money, and the market was waiting, because Britain already drank tea by the ton. Ceylon answered the rust with a new crop, paid for by people who could wait.

The rust spread across Asia and Africa and took a century to cross the Atlantic. It reached Brazil in 1970, and Chiapas, on Mexico's border with Guatemala, in 1981. Then in 2012, after a year when the nights stayed warm enough for the spores to keep growing, it exploded and hit about half the coffee land in Central America. Over the next three harvests, Mexico's crop fell from about 4.3 million bags to about 2.2 million.

Mexico couldn't do what Ceylon did. Its coffee is grown by about half a million families, and most of them farm two hectares or less, about three football pitches. Nobody builds a tea factory on three football pitches. So Mexico changed the tree instead, with the Timor crosses from our last episode, like Oro Azteca. They carry a sliver of robusta's genes, and its defence against rust.

The borrowed genes have a price. Mexico's own farm research institute rates their cup as low. In one tasting trial in Peru, a Catimor scored about eighty points against eighty-two for the Caturra beside it, and eighty is where specialty coffee starts. And the protection doesn't last. Costa Rica 95, one of the rescue varieties from our last episode, now catches rust in Costa Rica. The fungus changed faster than the tree.

Ceylon changed the crop, because its owners could afford three years without a harvest, and that answer is in this tin. Mexico's half a million families couldn't wait, so they changed the tree, with a little robusta folded in. That answer holds until the rust finds a way around it.
