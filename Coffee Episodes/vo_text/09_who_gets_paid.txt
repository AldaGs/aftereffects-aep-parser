This number changes every few seconds on a screen in New York. It's the price of a pound of coffee, in cents, and it sets what a family in Chiapas is paid for its harvest. A latte costs about five dollars. How much of that reaches the farmer, and who decides?

The coffee in a latte is about eighteen grams of roasted beans, which were about twenty-two grams of green coffee before the roast burned off about a sixth of their weight. In October 2026, a pound of green arabica traded for about three dollars in New York. Twenty-two grams of it comes to about fifteen cents, three percent of your five dollars.

That price is called the C price. It's set on the ICE futures exchange in New York, where traders buy and sell contracts for coffee to be delivered months from now. Most of them never touch a bean. When Brazil has a frost, the price jumps. When Brazil has a big harvest, it falls, and it falls in Chiapas too.

Then the price travels back up the hill, and something comes off at every stop. An exporter buys each shipment at the C price, plus or minus a differential. On the exchange, Mexican coffee counts at par; a real shipment sells above or below that, depending on quality. The exporter takes out milling, shipping, financing and a margin. A local buyer, the coyote, collects from farms the trucks can't reach. And the farmer is paid in pesos, per kilo of cherries, about six kilos for each kilo of green coffee. Official farm prices for 2020 came to roughly half to two-thirds of the New York price. Of your fifteen cents, that's about eight to ten.

A two-hectare farm in Chiapas grows about seven hundred kilos of green coffee a year, roughly twelve sacks. At the 2001 low, under fifty cents a pound, that crop was worth about seven hundred and seventy dollars at the New York price. In February 2025, at about four dollars, it was worth more than six thousand. The trees didn't change. New York did.

Governments once tried to take that swing away. From 1963, a treaty, the International Coffee Agreement, gave each exporting country a quota. When the price fell, quotas tightened and less coffee reached the market. The United States signed partly to keep Latin America's coffee economies steady during the Cold War. But the quotas ran on and off. They were suspended from 1972 to 1980, and again from 1986 to 1987, both times while frost or drought kept coffee dear. Both times, they came back.

In 1989 the members couldn't agree on new quotas, and on the fourth of July they suspended them again. Coffee that had been held back poured onto the market. Over the next five years the price averaged about seventy-seven cents a pound, against a dollar thirty-four in the five years before. This time, the quotas never came back.

Mexico lost a second floor the same year. Since 1958, a state agency, INMECAFE, had advised growers, and by the early 1980s it bought more than forty percent of the crop. In 1989 the government began pulling it out of the market, and by 1993 it was gone. A family on two hectares now had no treaty above it and no state buyer beside it. It had the coyote and the C price.

Some of those families had already built something. In the Isthmus of Oaxaca, a cooperative called UCIRI asked a Dutch charity for a fair price instead of aid. In November 1988 the result went on sale in the Netherlands: Max Havelaar, the first fair-trade label, with UCIRI's coffee in the pack. It guarantees a minimum, today a dollar eighty a pound for washed arabica, however low New York goes. In 2023, about two kilos in every hundred of the world's coffee sold that way. It's a floor where the treaty's used to be, for the farmers who can sell on it, and Mexican farmers drew it.

Most of your five dollars never leaves the café. About fifteen cents is the coffee, priced by traders in New York, and the farmer at the top of the hill gets around eight to ten of them. Since 1989, nothing holds that price up except a floor that started in Oaxaca.
