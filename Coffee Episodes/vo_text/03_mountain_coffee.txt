On a hillside above Coatepec, in Veracruz, there's a line nobody can see. Coffee picked above it, about twelve hundred metres up, can carry Mexico's top grade. Coffee a few rows below it can't, and buyers pay less for it. Why would the height of a farm set the grade of a bean?

The first thing height does is chill the air. Every hundred metres up, it cools by a little more than half a degree. Drive from the port of Veracruz up to Coatepec and the average day is about six degrees cooler: the difference between a sun hat and a jacket.

A coffee cherry ripens on a heat budget. From flower to harvest it needs a fixed amount of warmth, which agronomists count in degree-days. On a Colombian research farm at eleven hundred metres, averaging twenty-three degrees, the budget fills in about six months. At nineteen hundred metres and seventeen degrees, it takes closer to a year. Same plant. It just waits longer.

Those extra months go into the seed. As it fills, it packs its cells with oil and sugar for a seedling it will never grow, and thickens the walls between them. Fill slowly and the cells pack tighter. The seed comes out denser, and Mexican graders say they can spot one by eye: the cut down its middle closes into a zigzag.

On the island of Réunion, researchers grew arabica on sixteen plots and tasted the results. The weather that mattered most was the temperature while the seed was filling. Cooler plots tasted more acidic and more like fruit. Warmer plots built up compounds that taste of soil. The total sugar barely moved. The cold changes the mix, not the amount.

The trade turned that density into a grade. Guatemala calls its top coffee Strictly Hard Bean, from about thirteen hundred and fifty metres. Costa Rica uses the same name from about twelve hundred. Other countries say Strictly High Grown. Either way the claim is the same: this seed grew slowly. Colombia skips the altimeter and sorts its beans through sieves.

Mexico's grades come from the same idea. On one common scale, written down in a cuppers' manual in 1999, the ladder has four rungs, from Buen Lavado on the low farms to Estrictamente Altura, from about twelve hundred metres. Lavado means washed, which is a story of its own. Coatepec sits right on the top line, and that's where the metres start to mislead.

The bean never reads an altimeter. It only feels the temperature. Take two farms on that top line, both at twelve hundred metres: one above Coatepec, one in the Soconusco, in Chiapas. In one weather record, the Chiapas farm runs about a degree warmer, and its cherries fill their budget a couple of weeks sooner. To feel Coatepec's cold, it would have to sit about two hundred metres higher. And a tree can do a mountain's job. In a Costa Rican trial, shade delayed ripening by up to a month, without the farm moving at all. The grade counts metres. It can't see either of those.

Metres don't travel well. What a grade line really marks is how long the seed had to fill, and that's set by cold. Height is only the usual way to find it. And the cold is moving. Warm the air by one degree, and nineteen degrees climbs about a hundred and fifty metres up this hillside. The line stays where it is, and so does the name on the sacks.
