In 2000, Mexico gave Veracruz coffee a protected designation of origin, the way France protects Champagne. The declaration credits the coffee's acidity to two things. One is altitude, which is a story of its own. The other is a farm habit: letting the coffee ferment right after it's pulped. What is fermenting doing to a coffee bean?

Start with the cherry. Under the red skin is a sweet pulp. Under that is a layer of slime called mucilage, clinging to a papery shell, the parchment. Inside the parchment, wrapped in a thin silver skin, is the seed. Everything outside the seed has to go, and the mucilage is the hard part. It's about nine parts water. Most of the rest is sugar and pectin, the stuff that sets jam.

There's more than one way to deal with the slime. Picture a scale: how much of the fruit stays on the seed while it dries. Natural coffee, the oldest method, dries inside the whole cherry. Honey coffee keeps some of the mucilage, from a thin coat to nearly all of it. Newer methods, like fermenting in sealed tanks, change the air instead and need a scale of their own. Washed coffee keeps none of the fruit, and that's the end Veracruz's law has in mind.

On a washed farm, a pulping machine squeezes the seeds out of their skins on the day they're picked. The mucilage comes through with them, and water alone won't rinse it off. So the seeds go into a tank.

For about a day, the tank fills with life. Yeasts and lactic acid bacteria, relatives of the ones that sour yoghurt, multiply fastest. Together, the microbes eat about sixty percent of the simple sugars, and as they eat they make acid. The water goes from mildly sour to about as sour as tomato juice. Farmers check by hand. Early on, the seeds slip through their fingers. When they feel gritty, like wet gravel, the slime has let go.

For decades, the explanation was that the microbes digest the pectin, the way fruit rots. Around 2000, a French and Mexican team tested that on coffee from Coatepec. After twenty hours in the tank, the pectin was mostly still there. The bacterium that took over their tank, Leuconostoc, made no enzyme that cuts pectin. What tracked the slime coming loose was the acid. The team had two guesses why, the acid itself or water drawn out of the slime as its sugar disappears, and they called the exact cause unknown. The tank doesn't eat the mucilage. It sours, and the slime lets go.

Then the seeds are washed in channels of running water and spread to dry, on concrete patios or raised beds. A freshly washed seed is about half water, and it has to come down to between ten and twelve percent. Put a hundred grams out on the patio, and about fifty-six come back in.

Mexico's export coffee is mostly washed, and washing drinks water. A traditional mill can use up to about twenty litres for every kilo of cherry. The runoff, which Mexicans call aguas mieles, honey water, is sour and full of sugar, and as it rots in a stream it uses up the oxygen fish need. Some Mexican mills now scrub the mucilage off by machine, with about three and a half litres. But a machine skips the tank, and the tank is the step the law is proud of.

Fermenting does very little to the bean itself. It sours the water around it, and the slime comes loose. The law gives the tank credit for the acidity in your cup. The science can only vouch for the slime.
