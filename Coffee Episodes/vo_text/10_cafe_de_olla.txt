This kitchen is in Coatepec, in Veracruz, and the hillside outside the window is planted with coffee. The coffee on the table came out of a jar. Mexico grows coffee on about two-thirds of a million hectares, and most of what Mexicans drink is instant. How did a coffee country end up drinking powder?

Out of every ten cups drunk in Mexico, about six are instant. Those hectares would cover Mexico City four and a half times over. The coffee is here. The best of it was planted for somebody else.

Mexico planted coffee to sell abroad, and it still ships much of its best arabica out, most of it to the United States. For most of the twentieth century, the beans that made export grade went to the port, and the home market got what was left: the broken beans and the ones with defects. That's cheap coffee, and cheap coffee is what a factory needs.

The factory came from Switzerland. In 1930, Brazil, buried in unsold coffee, asked Nestlé to find a way to keep it. Nestlé's answer, in 1938, was Nescafé, coffee you dissolve. Nestlé now says it is the biggest buyer of coffee in Mexico, and it has called its plant in Toluca, outside Mexico City, its largest instant coffee factory.

Inside, the beans are roasted and ground, then packed into a line of tall steel columns. Water runs through, column after column, under pressure, so it can be heated far past boiling, to about a hundred and eighty degrees. At that heat, water dissolves things a kitchen pot can't. A pot pulls about a fifth of the weight out of the grounds. The columns pull out more than twice that. The liquid is concentrated, then dried, either sprayed into a tower of hot air or frozen and dried under vacuum. A teaspoon of powder, about two grams, makes a cup. It took about five grams of green beans. A cup from a pot takes about ten.

The factory doesn't need the best beans, or even Mexican ones. Robusta sells for about half the price of arabica and gives a strong cup. Mexico doesn't grow enough of it, so it buys much of it abroad, though that's changing as lowland farms plant more. This year, Mexico is expected to import more green coffee than it exports. In 2022 Nestlé opened a new plant in Veracruz, after growers there protested that it would push them to plant robusta.

Instant had an easy way in. The Mexican home cup was already built around something other than the bean. Café de olla is ground coffee simmered in a clay pot with piloncillo, raw cane sugar, and a stick of cinnamon. Mexicans tell a story that the women who followed the armies of the Revolution brewed it for the troops. The supermarket bags roasted with sugar, from our roasting episode, work the same way. When sugar and spice carry the flavour, the cheapest bean will do, and powder was the cheapest of all.

True cinnamon grows in Sri Lanka, the island that lost its coffee to rust in episode two. It sold cinnamon before it grew coffee, and it still does. Mexico is its biggest customer, buying about two-fifths of the cinnamon Sri Lanka sells abroad.

What's new is Mexicans buying their own coffee. After the price crashes, some growers stopped selling everything to the coyote and started roasting. Cafés selling beans from a named farm in Chiapas or Veracruz have spread through the cities, and roasted and ground coffee now makes up about two cups in five. The jar still wins. But beans that used to go to the port are being sold down the hill.

Mexico drinks instant because its best coffee was grown to leave, and because a cup sweetened with piloncillo and cinnamon never asked much of the bean. What's changing that is this bag, roasted from the hill outside the window.
