This is a coffee cherry. Inside are two seeds, and every cup you've ever had started as one of them. But not every cup comes from the same plant.

Coffee is a genus, Coffea, with more than a hundred and twenty wild species scattered across Africa, Madagascar and Asia. But almost all the coffee we drink comes from just two of them.

Coffea canephora, better known as robusta, lives up to its name. It grows low and hot, shrugs off pests, and carries about twice the caffeine, which makes it bitter. Coffea arabica is fussier. It wants altitude, cool nights and shade, and in return it's sweeter, brighter and more aromatic. Arabica is about sixty percent of the world's coffee. Robusta is almost all the rest.

That last sliver belongs to the smaller species. Liberica, a towering tree with big, smoky beans. Excelsa, tart and fruity, grown across Southeast Asia. Stenophylla, a species from Sierra Leone that was lost for decades, tastes like arabica, and handles the heat. And eugenioides, a mild highland plant that turns out to be arabica's mother.

Arabica is a natural hybrid of eugenioides and canephora, robusta itself, formed long before anyone drank coffee. It kept both sets of chromosomes, learned to pollinate itself, and grew wild in the forests of Ethiopia until people carried it across the Red Sea to Yemen.

From Yemen, almost every arabica on Earth descends from two lines. Typica travelled east to India and Java, then to Amsterdam, Paris and the Caribbean. Bourbon went south to the island of Bourbon, today Réunion, and on to Brazil and East Africa. Such a narrow family would matter later.

In the late seventeen hundreds, Typica reached Veracruz from the Caribbean. Today Mexico grows coffee in the mountains of Chiapas, Veracruz, Puebla and Oaxaca, and most of it is arabica.

Mexico's farms are a living family tree. Typica, known here as criollo, still grows on old farms. From it came Pluma Hidalgo, selected in Oaxaca, and Maragogype, a mutation with giant beans. Bourbon gave Caturra, a compact dwarf you can plant densely. Cross Typica with Bourbon and you get Mundo Novo, tall and productive. Cross Mundo Novo with Caturra and you get Catuaí, and, bred right here in Mexico, Garnica.

But a narrow family has a weak spot. In 2012, coffee leaf rust, la roya, swept through Central America and into Mexico, and harvests fell by around half. The rescue came from an old relative. On the island of Timor, arabica had crossed with robusta. Its rust-resistant descendants, Catimors and Sarchimors like Costa Rica 95, Oro Azteca and Marsellesa, now cover much of Mexico's hillsides.

So your next cup of Mexican coffee is almost certainly arabica, with a little robusta hiding in its family tree.
