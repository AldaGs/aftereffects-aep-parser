A honeybee is drinking from a coffee flower, and the nectar it's drinking contains caffeine. Caffeine is built from nitrogen, which the plant has to pull out of the soil. What is it spending that nitrogen on?

Caffeine isn't spread evenly through a coffee plant. An arabica seed is about one percent caffeine, a robusta seed about two. Young leaves are loaded with it. The nectar has only a trace, too little for a bee to taste.

The plant builds caffeine in four steps, bolting small carbon groups onto a cousin of DNA's building blocks. One step short of caffeine, it makes theobromine, the stimulant in chocolate. Tea makes caffeine too, and so do cacao, guarana and some citrus flowers. The coffee genome, published in 2014, showed that coffee's caffeine genes are its own, not inherited from an ancestor it shares with tea. A 2016 study counted at least five separate inventions. Evolution kept arriving at the same molecule, though it costs scarce nitrogen, which suggests it pays.

In 1984 a Harvard researcher, James Nathanson, fed caffeine to tobacco hornworm caterpillars. At doses like those in leaves, they stopped eating, and higher doses killed them. Inside an insect's nerve cell, every signal leaves a messenger behind. An enzyme clears it away, and the cell goes quiet. Caffeine sits in that enzyme's mouth. The messenger piles up, the cell keeps firing, and the caterpillar stops eating.

Caffeine works on rival plants too. Fallen leaves and seeds leach it into the soil, where it slows the roots of seedlings growing around the tree.

Robusta carries the most, about twice arabica's caffeine, and it grows in the hot lowlands, where insects and fungi are thick. That fit is the leading explanation, though nobody has proved it. In Mexico, robusta is about one bag in eight and growing, mostly in the lowlands of Chiapas, Veracruz, Puebla and Oaxaca.

So caffeine is a poison. But this bee isn't being poisoned. In 2013 a team led by Geraldine Wright gave honeybees sugar water, some of it laced with caffeine at nectar strength. A day later, the caffeinated bees were three times as likely to remember the flower's scent. After three days they were still twice as likely. At this dose, caffeine sharpens memory, through the same kind of receptor it blocks in your brain. A bee that remembers comes back, carrying pollen. The molecule doesn't change. The dose decides whether it's a weapon or a reward.

But the insect that does the most damage to coffee eats caffeine anyway. The coffee berry borer, la broca, crossed from Guatemala into Cacahoatán, Chiapas, in 1978. It's a beetle smaller than a sesame seed, and it spends its life inside the coffee seed, the most caffeinated part of the plant. In 2015 researchers sampled borers from seven coffee regions, Chiapas among them. Their guts carried bacteria that live on caffeine. Kill the bacteria with antibiotics and the beetles couldn't break caffeine down at all.

Mexico's answer was an animal caffeine can't stop. In 1988, researchers brought two species of tiny African wasp to Chiapas. They crawl into the cherry after the borer and lay their eggs on its young. One of the two settled in. The plant's chemistry keeps out the insects that eat everything. The one that got past it is now hunted by an insect that doesn't eat coffee at all.

Caffeine is the plant's defence, and the plant chooses the dose: enough in the seed to stop most insects, a trace in the nectar to bring the bee back. The broca evolved its way around the dose. The bee never had to.
